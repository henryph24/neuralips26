Raw-Routed Mixture of Adapters: A Causal Intervention for Routing Collapse in Time Series Foundation Models
Download PDF
Hung Phan, Thuy T. Nguyen, Minh Ngoc Dinh, Nhat-Quang Tran 
03 May 2026 (modified: 28 May 2026)
NeurIPS 2026 Conference Submission
Conference, Senior Area Chairs, Area Chairs, Reviewers, Authors
Revisions
CC BY 4.0
TL;DR: MoE adapters on instance-normalized time series foundation models collapse; eight causal controls isolate RevIN as the cause and prescribe raw-input routing (RR-MoA/SR-MoA/Residual-IA+), beating prior adapters and full fine-tuning.
Abstract:
Time series foundation models (TSFMs) commonly adapt to new data by attaching a single trainable head to a frozen backbone, a one-size-fits-all setup that underfits heterogeneous regimes. Replacing the head with a mixture of experts is the standard upgrade, but on instance-normalized backbones (the dominant TSFM design class) it fails: routing entropy collapses to zero and one expert absorbs every input, a failure we call normalization-induced routing collapse. Standard MoE rescue mechanisms do not repair it, because the cause is in the router's input, not its optimization. Pre-encoder normalization strips the statistics a router would need to tell regimes apart. A mutual-information decomposition makes this precise and yields a signal-ratio that, computed before training, predicts dataset vulnerability (Spearman 
). Eight causal controls, including a vision-modality replication, isolate instance normalization as the cause. The prescription is a minimal causal intervention: Raw-Routed Mixture of Adapters (RR-MoA), which routes on the raw, pre-normalization input. Under a strictly frozen backbone, RR-MoA wins 54/54 comparisons against the strongest fixed adapter and significantly outperforms LoRA, TRACE, AdaMix, and full fine-tuning. The effect generalizes across six backbones and an imputation task. Frozen RR-MoA also beats full fine-tuning by 12–79% (the Frozen Paradox); two architecturally distinct variants confirm the principle generalizes beyond this specific router. Code is provided in the supplementary material.

Official Reviewby Reviewer Pm4m26 Jun 2026, 17:14 (modified: 23 Jul 2026, 21:58)Program Chairs, Senior Area Chairs, Area Chairs, Reviewers Submitted, Authors, Reviewer Pm4mRevisions
Summary:
This paper studies why mixture of experts adapter heads can fail when adapting frozen time series foundation models. The authors identify normalization induced routing collapse, where a hidden state router collapses to a single expert within a few optimization steps when used with backbones that apply learnable affine instance normalization, such as RevIN in MOMENT. The paper argues that this failure is caused mainly by the router input rather than by the routing optimization itself, since RevIN removes per window mean and scale information that is important for distinguishing temporal regimes. This diagnosis is supported by a mutual information analysis, a training free signal ratio that correlates with collapse risk, and several causal controls, including normalization ablations, comparisons across backbones, and a replication in vision models. As a remedy, the authors propose RR-MoA, which routes using the raw input before normalization while keeping expert adaptation on frozen hidden states. The method is reported to outperform fixed adapters, LoRA, TRACE, AdaMix, and full fine tuning across all evaluated settings, with additional variants used to support generality across multiple backbones, forecasting horizons, and an imputation task.

Contribution Type: General: Most submissions will fall into this type.
Strengths And Weaknesses:
Strengths

S1 — The paper studies an interesting problem setting that is practically important.

S2 — The paper is logically structured, making its contributions easy to follow.

S3 — The authors conducted extensive experiments to demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed methodology.

Weaknesses

W1 — The abstract's "54/54 wins" and "beats full fine-tuning by 12–79%" are all measured against frozen-adapter / PEFT baselines, i.e., methods constrained to the same frozen-backbone regime the paper studies. Table 4, Appendix M, and Appendix P.3 show that the whole RR-MoA family is beaten in absolute MSE by a 49K-parameter DLinear (e.g., 0.680 vs 0.416 on ETTh1) and that the TSFM backbone is explicitly "dead weight" on several datasets. This is a material caveat that does not appear in the abstract or introduction. I would ask the authors to move the DLinear comparison and the backbone-contribution analysis into the main text and to reframe the contribution accordingly; as currently presented, the framing oversells a method whose absolute forecasting quality is below a trivial linear baseline.

W2 — Figure 2 is too simple and small; parts of it overlap, making it difficult to see clearly, and it fails to clearly illustrate the paper’s core modules and contributions.

W3 — The collapse mechanism predicts that backbones without learnable-affine encoder normalization should not collapse, and the paper confirms Moirai does not. Yet RR-MoA still "wins" 12–29% on Moirai. If there is no collapse to fix, this gain must come from something other than collapse-repair — most plausibly the predictive value of the raw signal itself, consistent with the Pure Raw-MLP result in P.3. This weakens the causal chain "collapse → raw routing is the fix," and the cross-backbone wins should not be presented as uniform validation of the diagnosis.

W4 — Closing the DLinear gap requires a 546-run sweep plus a stack of dataset-specific engineering choices (shared NLinear branch, b=−2 gate init, 5-epoch warmup, validation-based early stopping), after which the result is still only 5/6 match-or-beat at a −2.8% mean gap (ETTh1 still loses). Appendix P.3 then shows a backbone-free Raw-MLP MoE matches or beats the dual-stream variant on average, indicating the gains come largely from the raw/NLinear branch (essentially DLinear) rather than the TSFM. This makes Residual-IA+ read as heavy tuning to approach a linear baseline, and its generalization beyond these six benchmarks is unclear.

W5 — The (M,Σ)-centric account is in tension with the authors' own ablations. The sufficient-statistic router using only [μ,σ] degrades MSE by +48% (Appendix F), showing (μ,σ) is not sufficient, while temporal shuffling barely changes performance, indicating the router relies on order-invariant distributional content broader than (M,Σ). Since both Observation 1 and R(D) are built specifically around (M,Σ), the theory does not cleanly match the empirically operative signal.

W6 — Minor Issue:

Inconsistencies in router parameter counts: The main text, Fig. 2, and Table A.1 all list 853, while item 4 of the Checklist states ~1100.
An extra parenthesis is present in R(D) on line 146.
The statement “13–42% over RR-MoA on six datasets” on line 64 is incorrect, it should be over baselines.
The terms “finetuning” and “fine-tuning” are used inconsistently; they should be standardized to a single form.
Quality: 2: not good
Clarity: 2: not good
Significance: 3: good
Originality: 3: good
Questions:
The experiments use almost only 512 as input length and 96 as the forecasting horizon. If the input length and forecasting length change, do the methods and conclusion in the paper still hold?

α is not initialized inconsistently. Appendix K states that ℓ is initialized to 0, so α = σ(0) = 0.5, but afterward it says, “Initialize with 0.492 and decrease monotonically.” Where does this inconsistency come from?

If the author can address my concerns, I would raise my score.

Summary:
The routing distribution degenerates into a single-expert solution, with routing entropy approaching zero and one expert receiving nearly all inputs. We refer to this failure mode as normalization-induced routing collapse. Standard MoE rescue mechanisms do not repair it, because the cause is in the router’s input, not its optimization. This paper proposes Raw-Routed Mixture of Adapters (RR-MoA), which routes on the raw, pre-normalization input. This is a minimal causal intervention. Under a strictly frozen backbone, RRMoA wins 54/54 comparisons against the strongest fixed adapter and significantly outperforms LoRA, TRACE, AdaMix, and full fine-tuning. The effect generalizes across six backbones and an imputation task. Frozen RR-MoA also beats full fine-tuning by 12–79% (the Frozen Paradox); two architecturally distinct variants confirm the principle generalizes beyond this specific router.

Contribution Type: Use-inspired: The main contribution is in framing or designing approaches to meet the needs of a specific real-world application. (This often involves, e.g., engaging with domain experts.)
Strengths And Weaknesses:
Strengths The experimental evaluation covers six representative long-term time-series forecasting (LTSF) benchmarks, providing evidence across a diverse set of datasets.

The paper identifies a plausible causal mechanism, termed normalization-induced routing collapse, and proposes a simple yet targeted intervention that directly addresses the issue without introducing substantial architectural complexity.

The proposed signal-relation metric appears capable of predicting dataset vulnerability prior to training, which could provide practical value beyond the specific method introduced in the paper.

The experiments provide empirical evidence for the existence of routing collapse and demonstrate the effectiveness of RR-MoA in mitigating this phenomenon.

Weaknesses Since the router operates directly on raw inputs, bypassing input normalization, the approach may be less memory-efficient than conventional designs. The implications for GPU memory usage and computational efficiency are not fully discussed.

The theoretical analysis focuses primarily on the interaction between instance normalization and router inputs. It remains unclear to what extent the proposed explanation accounts for other factors, such as dataset-specific distributional properties or feature asymmetries.

The scalability of the approach deserves further investigation. As the number of experts increases, the dimensionality of the router input and the associated memory bandwidth requirements may become a bottleneck.

The causal analysis is conducted mainly through interventions on instance normalization. It is therefore unclear whether similar routing-collapse phenomena arise under other normalization schemes, such as BatchNorm, LayerNorm, or RMSNorm.

The experimental evaluation is limited to forecasting and missing-value imputation tasks. The applicability of the proposed insights to other settings, such as classification or generative modeling, remains unexplored.

Some experiments appear to use a relatively small number of training epochs. It would be helpful to provide additional evidence that the reported results are not affected by incomplete convergence.

The statistical analysis may be sensitive to the choice of weighting schemes and multiple-testing corrections. If these procedures are overly conservative, they could reduce statistical power and potentially obscure meaningful effects.

Quality: 2: not good
Clarity: 2: not good
Significance: 2: not good
Originality: 3: good
Questions:
The paper attributes routing collapse primarily to the use of normalized inputs for routing decisions. Have the authors considered applying the proposed raw-routing strategy to existing MoE architectures, such as AdaMix or related variants? Such an experiment would help clarify whether the routing input itself is the dominant source of the problem, or whether other architectural factors also contribute to routing collapse.

The causal analysis is based on eight intervention settings, including RevIN ablation and BatchNorm swap. Do the authors expect the same causal mechanisms and intervention effects to hold in other tasks or domains, such as image modeling? Additional discussion on the generality of the proposed explanation would help assess whether the identified mechanism is specific to LTSF or reflects a broader phenomenon in MoE architectures.

Several experiments appear to be conducted using relatively short training schedules. Have the authors evaluated the method under longer training regimes (200 epochs or beyond)? It would be useful to understand whether the reported advantages persist after full convergence and whether longer training affects routing stability or performance.

Limitations:
The empirical analysis focuses primarily on instance-normalization methods that remove sample-specific statistics, such as RevIN and RMSNorm. It remains unclear whether similar routing-collapse phenomena would arise under other normalization schemes (BatchNorm or GroupNorm), which may limit the generality of the proposed explanation. The experiments consider forecasting horizons up to 720 time steps. It is therefore unclear whether the same conclusions hold for substantially longer temporal horizons, where internal representations and routing behaviors may become more diverse. The study employs a fixed set of relatively simple adapters (linear, attention-based, and convolutional adapters). The behavior of the proposed method in settings with learned, dynamically generated, or substantially larger adapter pools remains unexplored. Consequently, the scalability and robustness of the conclusions to more complex routing scenarios are not yet established.

Summary:
The routing distribution degenerates into a single-expert solution, with routing entropy approaching zero and one expert receiving nearly all inputs. We refer to this failure mode as normalization-induced routing collapse. Standard MoE rescue mechanisms do not repair it, because the cause is in the router’s input, not its optimization. This paper proposes Raw-Routed Mixture of Adapters (RR-MoA), which routes on the raw, pre-normalization input. This is a minimal causal intervention. Under a strictly frozen backbone, RRMoA wins 54/54 comparisons against the strongest fixed adapter and significantly outperforms LoRA, TRACE, AdaMix, and full fine-tuning. The effect generalizes across six backbones and an imputation task. Frozen RR-MoA also beats full fine-tuning by 12–79% (the Frozen Paradox); two architecturally distinct variants confirm the principle generalizes beyond this specific router.

Contribution Type: Use-inspired: The main contribution is in framing or designing approaches to meet the needs of a specific real-world application. (This often involves, e.g., engaging with domain experts.)
Strengths And Weaknesses:
Strengths The experimental evaluation covers six representative long-term time-series forecasting (LTSF) benchmarks, providing evidence across a diverse set of datasets.

The paper identifies a plausible causal mechanism, termed normalization-induced routing collapse, and proposes a simple yet targeted intervention that directly addresses the issue without introducing substantial architectural complexity.

The proposed signal-relation metric appears capable of predicting dataset vulnerability prior to training, which could provide practical value beyond the specific method introduced in the paper.

The experiments provide empirical evidence for the existence of routing collapse and demonstrate the effectiveness of RR-MoA in mitigating this phenomenon.

Weaknesses Since the router operates directly on raw inputs, bypassing input normalization, the approach may be less memory-efficient than conventional designs. The implications for GPU memory usage and computational efficiency are not fully discussed.

The theoretical analysis focuses primarily on the interaction between instance normalization and router inputs. It remains unclear to what extent the proposed explanation accounts for other factors, such as dataset-specific distributional properties or feature asymmetries.

The scalability of the approach deserves further investigation. As the number of experts increases, the dimensionality of the router input and the associated memory bandwidth requirements may become a bottleneck.

The causal analysis is conducted mainly through interventions on instance normalization. It is therefore unclear whether similar routing-collapse phenomena arise under other normalization schemes, such as BatchNorm, LayerNorm, or RMSNorm.

The experimental evaluation is limited to forecasting and missing-value imputation tasks. The applicability of the proposed insights to other settings, such as classification or generative modeling, remains unexplored.

Some experiments appear to use a relatively small number of training epochs. It would be helpful to provide additional evidence that the reported results are not affected by incomplete convergence.

The statistical analysis may be sensitive to the choice of weighting schemes and multiple-testing corrections. If these procedures are overly conservative, they could reduce statistical power and potentially obscure meaningful effects.

Quality: 2: not good
Clarity: 2: not good
Significance: 2: not good
Originality: 3: good
Questions:
The paper attributes routing collapse primarily to the use of normalized inputs for routing decisions. Have the authors considered applying the proposed raw-routing strategy to existing MoE architectures, such as AdaMix or related variants? Such an experiment would help clarify whether the routing input itself is the dominant source of the problem, or whether other architectural factors also contribute to routing collapse.

The causal analysis is based on eight intervention settings, including RevIN ablation and BatchNorm swap. Do the authors expect the same causal mechanisms and intervention effects to hold in other tasks or domains, such as image modeling? Additional discussion on the generality of the proposed explanation would help assess whether the identified mechanism is specific to LTSF or reflects a broader phenomenon in MoE architectures.

Several experiments appear to be conducted using relatively short training schedules. Have the authors evaluated the method under longer training regimes (200 epochs or beyond)? It would be useful to understand whether the reported advantages persist after full convergence and whether longer training affects routing stability or performance.

Limitations:
The empirical analysis focuses primarily on instance-normalization methods that remove sample-specific statistics, such as RevIN and RMSNorm. It remains unclear whether similar routing-collapse phenomena would arise under other normalization schemes (BatchNorm or GroupNorm), which may limit the generality of the proposed explanation. The experiments consider forecasting horizons up to 720 time steps. It is therefore unclear whether the same conclusions hold for substantially longer temporal horizons, where internal representations and routing behaviors may become more diverse. The study employs a fixed set of relatively simple adapters (linear, attention-based, and convolutional adapters). The behavior of the proposed method in settings with learned, dynamically generated, or substantially larger adapter pools remains unexplored. Consequently, the scalability and robustness of the conclusions to more complex routing scenarios are not yet established.
