\begin{abstract}
Standard mixture-of-experts (MoE) routing on frozen time series foundation models (TSFMs) collapses catastrophically: routing entropy drops to $0.000{\pm}0.000$ on backbones with Reversible Instance Normalization (RevIN) when layers are unfrozen. We diagnose the cause as \textbf{normalization-induced co-adaptation}---RevIN strips per-window mean and scale, while gradient feedback amplifies the collapse. A controlled ablation disabling RevIN inside MOMENT confirms causality; a cross-backbone control on Moirai (no RevIN) shows no collapse. We formalize this via a mutual-information decomposition (Proposition~1), predicting that datasets with higher location-scale variance benefit more from raw routing---validated at Spearman $\rho{=}{-}0.96$ ($p{<}0.001$) across seven datasets, including correctly predicting where RR-MoA does not help.
To break the collapse, we propose \textbf{Raw-Routed Mixture of Adapters (RR-MoA)}: a lightweight gate routes on the raw pre-normalization input while a frozen backbone produces embeddings for the experts. RR-MoA achieves \textbf{54/54 wins} across 6 datasets, 3 freeze levels, and 3 seeds ($p{<}0.001$, Wilcoxon), outperforming LoRA, TRACE, and independent ensembles. On Moirai, RR-MoA matches DLinear on Weather ($0.209$ vs $0.208$). We uncover a \textbf{Frozen Paradox}: frozen routing outperforms unfreezing on 4/6 datasets, reversing the standard PEFT heuristic.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The transformer architecture~\citep{vaswani2017attention} has enabled foundation models~\citep{bommasani2021foundation} across language~\citep{devlin2019bert, brown2020gpt3} and vision~\citep{dosovitskiy2021vit}. Time series foundation models (TSFMs) such as MOMENT~\citep{goswami2024moment}, TimesFM~\citep{das2024timesfm}, Chronos~\citep{ansari2024chronos}, Timer/Timer-XL~\citep{liu2024timer, liu2025timerxl}, Moirai/Moirai-MoE~\citep{woo2024moirai, liu2025moiraimoe}, UniTS~\citep{lee2024units}, Lag-Llama~\citep{rasul2024lagllama}, Time-MoE~\citep{shi2024timemoe}, and TTM~\citep{ekambaram2024ttm} have emerged as powerful pretrained representations for temporal tasks. Once a TSFM is pretrained, a concrete practical question determines whether it is useful in production: \textit{how should the adapter that connects it to a downstream task be designed and trained?}

\textbf{The one-size-fits-all adapter status quo.} Current TSFM adaptation almost always reduces to the same computational template: \emph{pool the backbone's hidden states $\to$ project to the output horizon}, with a handful of standard variants---linear probing~\citep{goswami2024moment, das2024timesfm}, MLP heads~\citep{nie2023patchtst, wu2023timesnet}, LoRA~\citep{hu2022lora}, and bottleneck adapters~\citep{houlsby2019adapters}. Crucially, every public TSFM benchmark we are aware of uses a \emph{single static} adapter for an entire dataset. Yet real time series are highly non-stationary: a dataset such as ETTh1 mixes quiet baseline segments with sharp seasonal excursions, and it is far from obvious that a single adapter topology is optimal for every window. This observation motivates a \emph{mixture of adapters}: maintain a small pool of topologically distinct expert heads and let a learned router assign each window to the most appropriate expert per sample.

\textbf{Why frozen at all?} Lightweight supervised baselines (DLinear~\citep{zeng2023dlinear}) achieve materially lower absolute MSE than any frozen-backbone adapter on the same evaluation scale. Our contribution is not to replace single-dataset specialists but to optimize routing within the frozen-backbone paradigm motivated by (i)~multi-tenant cloud serving with hot-swappable per-tenant adapters, (ii)~on-device deployment where per-site retraining is infeasible, and (iii)~cross-task reuse where one backbone drives forecasting and imputation via different expert pools (Appendix~\ref{app:deployment}). As TSFMs scale toward billion-parameter regimes~\citep{shi2024timemoe}, frozen-backbone PEFT becomes the only viable per-dataset adaptation path.

\textbf{The natural idea that fails: MoE on TSFMs.} When we apply the standard MoE recipe~\citep{wang2022adamix, fedus2022switch}---routing on hidden states with load balancing---to a TSFM with internal RevIN, it \emph{collapses catastrophically}. Across 3 seeds, 3 datasets, and every setting where backbone layers are unfrozen, routing entropy drops to exactly $0.000\pm0.000$ (Table~\ref{tab:adamix}): all weight concentrates on a single expert.

\textbf{Diagnosis: normalization-induced co-adaptation.} The collapse has a specific causal structure: whichever expert the router marginally prefers receives larger gradients; those flow back into the unfrozen backbone, which reshapes $\mathbf{H}$ to serve that expert better, completing a positive feedback loop within ${\sim}50$ steps (Figure~\ref{fig:trajectory}). Freezing $\theta$ breaks this loop (Table~\ref{tab:adamix}: entropy recovers to $0.5$--$0.6$), but hidden-state routing still underperforms because the backbone's internal RevIN~\citep{kim2021revin} strips the temporal statistics (trend, amplitude, volatility) that would distinguish samples. A controlled ablation (Table~\ref{tab:router_input}) confirms this: applying RevIN to the router's input degrades MSE by $60$--$88\%$. A cross-backbone control on Moirai~\citep{woo2024moirai} (which lacks RevIN) shows that AdaMix routing \emph{does not collapse} even with unfrozen layers (entropy $1.48$--$1.60$; \S\ref{sec:cross_backbone}), confirming that the failure is normalization-specific, not inherent to MoE on temporal data.

\textbf{Our fix.} We propose \textbf{Raw-Routed Mixture of Adapters (RR-MoA)}: (i)~the router reads the raw, pre-normalization input $\mathbf{X}_\text{raw}$ (preserving per-window trend, amplitude, volatility), and (ii)~the backbone is kept strictly frozen ($\texttt{requires\_grad}{=}\texttt{False}$ for all $\theta$). Only the router and experts are trainable, reducing the optimization from $\theta_\text{active}\cup\phi\cup\psi$ to $\phi\cup\psi$ alone.

\textbf{The Frozen Paradox.} In our 3\,datasets\,$\times$\,3\,freeze levels\,$\times$\,3\,seeds ablation (Table~\ref{tab:rrmoa}), strictly frozen RR-MoA is at least as good as partially unfrozen RR-MoA on every dataset, with $43.5\%$ and $51.1\%$ MSE reductions on ETTh1 and ETTm1. Unfreezing buys routing collapse, not expressivity---reversing the standard NLP PEFT heuristic.

\textbf{Expert pool.} RR-MoA uses five canonical adapter heads (mean-pool, last-token, max-pool, attention-pool, Conv1d-pool). An LLM-guided architecture search (Appendix~\ref{app:aas_details}) confirms that richer code-level search spaces contain strictly better single adapters ($-9\%$ MSE; Appendix~\ref{app:aas_results}), motivating expert diversity.

Our contributions:

\textbf{(1)~Diagnosis and causal proof.} We identify \emph{normalization-induced co-adaptation} as the cause of MoE routing collapse on TSFMs: entropy $0.000\pm0.000$ whenever backbone layers are unfrozen. Three converging controls establish causality: (a)~Moirai (no RevIN) does not collapse (Table~\ref{tab:adamix}, \S\ref{sec:cross_backbone}); (b)~disabling RevIN inside MOMENT recovers routing entropy; (c)~applying RevIN to the router's input degrades MSE by $60$--$88\%$ (Table~\ref{tab:router_input}).

\textbf{(2)~RR-MoA and the Frozen Paradox.} We propose \textbf{RR-MoA}, which routes on the raw pre-normalization signal with a frozen backbone, achieving \textbf{54/54 wins} across 6 datasets, 3 freeze levels, and 3 seeds, significantly outperforming LoRA, TRACE-style adapters, and independent ensembles ($p{<}0.001$, Wilcoxon; Table~\ref{tab:baselines}). Strictly frozen routing outperforms partial unfreezing on 4/6 datasets---the \textbf{Frozen Paradox}---reversing the standard PEFT heuristic. RR-MoA generalizes across three backbones (MOMENT-small/large, Moirai), with Moirai+RR-MoA matching DLinear on Weather (Appendix~\ref{app:cross_backbone}).

\textbf{(3)~Formal theory with predictive power.} We prove an information-theoretic decomposition (Proposition~\ref{thm:mi_decomp}) showing that RevIN destroys routing mutual information equal to the information in the stripped location-scale statistics. This yields a quantitative prediction: datasets with higher location-scale variance should show larger RR-MoA gains---validated with Spearman $\rho{=}{-}0.96$ ($p{<}0.001$) across seven datasets, including correctly predicting where RR-MoA does \emph{not} help (Figure~\ref{fig:signal_ratio}).

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{TSFM Adaptation.} Standard adaptation uses LoRA~\citep{hu2022lora} or bottleneck adapters~\citep{houlsby2019adapters} with manually chosen configurations. MSFT~\citep{qiao2025msft} proposes multi-scale finetuning for encoder-based TSFMs. TRACE~\citep{li2025trace} introduces importance-based LoRA selection with reconstructed heads---the closest work, but heads are hand-designed. Beyond LoRA~\citep{gupta2024beyondlora} surveys PEFT alternatives for Chronos. AdaPTS~\citep{benechehab2025adapts} adapts input representations. Prune-then-Finetune~\citep{zhao2025prune} applies structured pruning. In-context fine-tuning~\citep{faw2025icf} adapts at inference time. TEMPLATE~\citep{zhang2025template} addresses model selection; GIFT-Eval~\citep{woo2024gifteval} provides benchmarking. All search over finetuning \textit{strategies}, not adapter \textit{architecture}---the gap we address.

\textbf{MoE in Foundation Models.} Mixture-of-experts routing~\citep{fedus2022switch} has been applied within TSFM backbones: Moirai-MoE~\citep{liu2025moiraimoe} routes frequency bands across internal experts, and Time-MoE~\citep{shi2024timemoe} scales to billions of parameters via sparse activation. These apply MoE \emph{within} the backbone architecture. In contrast, RR-MoA applies MoE at the \emph{adapter level} on a frozen backbone---an orthogonal and complementary design axis. AdaMix~\citep{wang2022adamix} introduces adapter mixtures for NLP but routes on hidden states, which we show collapses on TSFMs with RevIN. No prior work has identified normalization-induced routing collapse or proposed pre-normalization routing.

\textbf{Normalization in Time Series.} RevIN~\citep{kim2021revin} applies reversible instance normalization to mitigate distribution shift. DishTS~\citep{fan2023dishts} and Non-stationary Transformers~\citep{liu2022nonstationary} address normalization from the forecasting accuracy perspective. We identify a previously unknown side effect: RevIN destroys the per-window statistics that MoE routers require for discriminative gating, creating a fundamental tension between normalization for forecasting and normalization for routing.

\textbf{NAS and LLM-Guided Search.} NAS~\citep{zoph2017nas} has evolved through ENAS~\citep{pham2018enas}, DARTS~\citep{liu2019darts}, and regularized evolution~\citep{real2019regularized}. Shears~\citep{munoz2024shears} and RZ-NAS~\citep{ji2025rznas} search adapter hyperparameters for LLMs; NAS-LoRA~\citep{chen2025naslora} targets vision adapters. FunSearch~\citep{romera2024funsearch}, EvoPrompting~\citep{chen2023evoprompting}, PartEvo~\citep{hu2025partevo}, and ReEvo~\citep{ye2024reevo} demonstrate LLM-guided program synthesis. We use this paradigm for expert pool construction (Appendix~\ref{app:aas_details}).

\subsection{Problem Formulation and Expert Pool}

An \emph{adapter} $g_\phi: \mathbb{R}^{B \times T \times d} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^{B \times H}$ maps a pretrained backbone's hidden states to forecasts, with $n_g \leq 500{,}000$ trainable parameters. We maintain a pool of $K{=}5$ topologically distinct expert adapters (mean-pool, last-token, max-pool, attention-pool, Conv1d-pool). An LLM-guided architecture search over arbitrary PyTorch \texttt{nn.Module} programs (Appendix~\ref{app:aas_details}) confirms that richer search spaces yield strictly better single adapters ($-9\%$ MSE; Appendix~\ref{app:aas_results}), motivating expert diversity. Formal definitions, search space hierarchy, and grammar are in Appendices~\ref{app:formal_defs}--\ref{app:grammar}.

\textbf{Standard protocol.} Chronological train/val/test splits per the LTSF benchmark~\citep{zhou2021informer}: ETT-hourly (8640/2880/2880), ETT-minutely (34560/11520/11520), Weather/Electricity (60/20/20\%). Final evaluation uses the held-out test set.

\subsection{Raw-Routed Mixture of Adapters (RR-MoA)}
\label{sec:rrmoa}

Standard MoE routing on TSFM hidden states collapses catastrophically due to gradient co-adaptation (\S\ref{sec:intro}). We propose \textbf{RR-MoA}, which splits the information stream: the TSFM processes deep embeddings $\mathbf{H}$, while a lightweight gate reads the \textit{raw, pre-normalization} input $\mathbf{X}_\text{raw}$ to compute sparse Top-$k$ routing weights:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{Y} = \sum_{j \in \mathcal{T}} \underbrace{\widetilde{w}_j}_{\text{sparse weight}} \cdot \underbrace{\text{Expert}_j(\mathbf{H}_\text{FM})}_{\text{deep embedding branch}},\quad
\mathcal{T} = \mathrm{TopK}\!\left(G_\psi(\mathbf{X}_\text{raw}),\, k\right),\;\;
\widetilde{w} = \mathrm{softmax}_{\mathcal{T}}\!\left(G_\psi(\mathbf{X}_\text{raw})\right)
\label{eq:rrmoa}
\end{equation}
where $G_\psi$ is a small Conv1d\,+\,pooling\,+\,linear gate ($\sim$1.1K params) operating directly on the raw time series, and each $\text{Expert}_k$ is a canonical adapter head. Routing is restricted to the Top-$k$ experts with the highest gate logits, so only $k$ of $K$ experts execute per sample. With $K{=}5$, $k{=}2$ we obtain $40\%$ of the dense FLOPs while retaining $63\%$ of the dense MSE gain (\S\ref{sec:topk}). The raw input preserves the temporal statistics (trend, amplitude, volatility) that RevIN strips away, enabling per-sample routing that reflects the physical characteristics of each time series window.

\begin{algorithm}[h]
\caption{RR-MoA: Raw-Routed Mixture of Adapters with Top-$k$ sparse routing}
\label{alg:rrmoa}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE Strictly frozen TSFM $f_{\theta_\text{frozen}}$, raw input $\mathbf{X}_\text{raw}\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times L}$, $K$ expert adapters, sparsity $k\leq K$
\STATE $\mathbf{H}\leftarrow f_{\theta_\text{frozen}}(\mathbf{X}_\text{raw})$ \hfill \COMMENT{Deep embeddings; \textbf{no gradients} through $\theta$}
\STATE $\mathbf{g}\leftarrow G_\psi(\mathbf{X}_\text{raw})$ \hfill \COMMENT{Raw-signal gate logits, $\mathbf{g}\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times K}$}
\STATE $\mathcal{T}\leftarrow \mathrm{TopK}(\mathbf{g},\,k)$ \hfill \COMMENT{Per-sample indices of the $k$ selected experts}
\STATE $\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}\leftarrow \mathrm{softmax}_{\mathcal{T}}(\mathbf{g})$ \hfill \COMMENT{Renormalized over selected experts only}
\STATE \textbf{for each}\ $j \in \mathcal{T}$\ \textbf{do}
\STATE \quad $\mathbf{o}_j\leftarrow \text{Expert}_j(\mathbf{H})$ \hfill \COMMENT{Only the $k$ selected experts execute}
\STATE \textbf{end for}
\STATE $\mathbf{Y}\leftarrow \sum_{j\in\mathcal{T}}\widetilde{w}_j\cdot \mathbf{o}_j$
\RETURN $\mathbf{Y}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Experiments}

\subsection{Setup}

\textbf{Backbones.} Our primary backbone is MOMENT-small~\citep{goswami2024moment} ($d_\text{model}{=}512$, 8 T5-style encoder blocks, internal RevIN~\citep{kim2021revin}). We additionally evaluate MOMENT-large ($d_\text{model}{=}1024$, 24 blocks) and Moirai-1.1-R-small~\citep{woo2024moirai} ($d_\text{model}{=}384$, 6 blocks, no RevIN), testing cross-backbone generalization in \S\ref{sec:cross_backbone}. Primary results use a \emph{strictly frozen} backbone ($\theta$ not updated); freeze-level ablations appear in \S\ref{sec:freeze_ablation}.

\textbf{MSE scale.} All MSE values are reported on channel-wise StandardScaler-normalized inputs, the standard LTSF convention~\citep{zhou2021informer, nie2023patchtst}. As a calibration anchor, DLinear~\citep{zeng2023dlinear} on our pipeline achieves $0.416$ (ETTh1), $0.322$ (ETTm1), $0.208$ (Weather)---consistent with published numbers (Appendix~\ref{app:dlinear}).

\textbf{Datasets and tasks.} ETTh1, ETTh2, ETTm1, ETTm2~\citep{zhou2021informer}, Weather (21 channels), and Electricity (321 channels)---standard LTSF benchmarks~\citep{godahewa2021monash, wen2023tssurvey}. Chronological train/val/test splits follow the LTSF protocol. We evaluate both long-term forecasting ($H{=}96$) and masked imputation (20\% reconstruction).

\textbf{Training.} Adam optimizer, lr$=10^{-3}$, MSE loss, batch size 128, 15 epochs. Adapter architecture search (during AAS) uses 3-epoch fitness probes on the validation split; final reported numbers are 15-epoch test MSE. Multi-seed experiments use seeds $\{42, 43, 44\}$.

\textbf{Baselines.} We compare against six baselines under the identical strictly frozen protocol: (i)~three hand-designed \textit{fixed adapters} (linear, attention, conv---representing standard TSFM practice), (ii)~\textit{LoRA}~\citep{hu2022lora} (108-run sweep over rank, targets, and heads; Appendix~\ref{app:lora_sweep}), (iii)~a \textit{TRACE-style} multi-scale adapter~\citep{li2025trace} (multi-scale convolutions + gated importance weighting; no public code available, we implement the core ideas), (iv)~an \textit{independent ensemble} of the same 5 expert heads trained separately and averaged at inference (isolating routing from expert diversity), (v)~\textit{AdaMix}~\citep{wang2022adamix} (standard hidden-state MoE routing), and (vi)~\textit{DLinear}~\citep{zeng2023dlinear} as a supervised calibration anchor (not a competitor). All frozen-backbone baselines use identical training setup.

\textbf{Reproducibility.} All code, raw JSON results, per-seed routing-weight logs, and a self-verification script (\texttt{verify.py}) that re-derives every numerical claim from raw data are included in the supplementary material and will be open-sourced upon acceptance.

\textbf{The Frozen Paradox.} Across all 6 datasets (Appendix~\ref{app:extended_rrmoa}), strictly frozen is the best configuration on 4 and within $13\%$ on the remaining 2, \emph{reversing} the standard PEFT heuristic. The mechanism is Table~\ref{tab:adamix}: unfreezing destroys routing diversity. \begin{proposition}[Gradient Co-Adaptation Drives Entropy Collapse]
\label{prop:frozen}
Consider a $2$-expert MoE with backbone $\mathbf{h}{=}A\mathbf{x}$, router $p{=}\sigma(\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{h})$, loss $\ell{=}p\,\ell_1{+}(1{-}p)\,\ell_2$. Under gradient descent on $(A,\mathbf{w})$: \textup{(i)} the dominant expert's loss decreases faster ($\dot{\ell}_k \propto {-}p_k\|\nabla_\mathbf{h}\ell_k\|^2$), creating self-reinforcing collapse toward $p{\in}\{0,1\}$; \textup{(ii)} freezing $A$ breaks this loop (proof in Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}).
\end{proposition}

\textbf{Routing vs.\ ensembling.} Replacing the learned router with a fixed uniform mixture ($w_k = 1/K$) degrades performance by $30$--$75\%$ on every dataset (ETTh1: $0.899$ vs $0.690$; ETTm1: $1.002$ vs $0.572$; Weather: $0.494$ vs $0.289$). Training the same 5 experts \emph{independently} and averaging predictions (Table~\ref{tab:baselines}, ``Ind.\ Ensemble'') is $36$--$46\%$ worse than RR-MoA, confirming that learned per-sample routing---not merely expert diversity---is the source of the gains.

\textbf{Why RevIN destroys routing: formal analysis.} Each input window $\mathbf{x}_i$ decomposes into mean $M_i = \mu(\mathbf{x}_i)$, scale $\Sigma_i = \sigma(\mathbf{x}_i)$, and shape $\mathbf{S}_i = (\mathbf{x}_i - M_i)/\Sigma_i$. RevIN maps $\mathbf{x}_i \mapsto \mathbf{S}_i$, discarding $(M_i, \Sigma_i)$.

\begin{proposition}[Routing Information Loss Under RevIN]
\label{thm:mi_decomp}
Let $X$ be a random input window, $E \in \{1,\ldots,K\}$ the expert assignment produced by any deterministic router, and $S = (X - M)/\Sigma$ the RevIN-normalized shape.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item[\textup{(i)}] $I(X; E) \geq I(S; E)$ \hfill\textup{(Data Processing Inequality)}
    \item[\textup{(ii)}] If $(M, \Sigma) \perp\!\!\!\perp S$, then $I(X; E) - I(S; E) = I(M, \Sigma;\, E)$.
    \item[\textup{(iii)}] If additionally $E \perp\!\!\!\perp S \mid (M, \Sigma)$, then $I(X; E) - I(S; E) = H(E)$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

Part~(i) is the DPI applied to $X \to S \to E$. Part~(ii) uses the chain rule: $I(X;E) = I(S;E) + I(M,\Sigma; E \mid S)$, which equals $I(S;E) + I(M,\Sigma; E)$ under independence. Part~(iii) shows that when routing depends \emph{entirely} on the stripped statistics, RevIN destroys \emph{all} routing information ($H(E)$ nats). Full proof in Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}. The independence $(M,\Sigma) \perp\!\!\!\perp S$ is RevIN's design assumption; $E \perp\!\!\!\perp S \mid (M,\Sigma)$ is validated by Figure~\ref{fig:routing_viz}, where expert selection clusters by amplitude and volatility regardless of waveform shape.

\textbf{Quantitative prediction.} Proposition~\ref{thm:mi_decomp} implies that datasets where RevIN strips more information---i.e., where $\text{Var}(M, \Sigma)$ is large relative to $\text{Var}(S)$---should show larger RR-MoA improvements. We define the \emph{routing signal ratio} $R(\mathcal{D}) = [\text{Var}(M) + \text{Var}(\Sigma)] / \overline{\text{Var}(S)}$ and compute it for each dataset. Figure~\ref{fig:signal_ratio} validates this prediction across all seven LTSF benchmarks: $R$ correlates with the RR-MoA improvement $\Delta\%$ with Spearman $\rho = -0.96$ ($p < 0.001$). Crucially, Traffic---the dataset with the lowest $R{=}0.14$---is correctly predicted as the boundary case where raw routing provides no advantage ($\Delta{=}{+}2.9\%$), demonstrating that the theory identifies not only \emph{where} raw routing helps but also \emph{where it does not}.

This explains all empirical observations: (i)~the RevIN router loses $60$--$88\%$ MSE because it loses $I(M,\Sigma; E)$; (ii)~Moirai (no RevIN) retains routing diversity because the gate accesses $(M, \Sigma, S)$; (iii)~Figure~\ref{fig:routing_viz} confirms expert selection correlates with amplitude and volatility. When backbone layers are unfrozen, the co-adaptation loop (Figure~\ref{fig:trajectory}) amplifies the signal poverty: the dominant expert's gradient grows while others starve, collapsing to a single expert within ${\sim}50$ steps.

\textbf{What the router learns.} Figure~\ref{fig:routing_viz} visualizes per-sample expert selection against temporal statistics. On ETTh1, Expert~0 (mean-pool) serves as the generalist ($53\%$ of windows), while Expert~2 (max-pool) preferentially handles low-amplitude, low-volatility windows (amp${\approx}1.2$, vol${\approx}0.06$) and Expert~3 (attention-pool) handles high-amplitude, high-volatility windows (amp${\approx}5.1$, vol${\approx}0.50$). The router reserves specialized processing for windows at the tails of the distribution. On Weather, the distribution shifts: Expert~2 dominates ($69\%$), consistent with Weather's lower overall volatility. Expert selection correlates with amplitude and volatility---the very statistics RevIN would strip---confirming the information-theoretic argument above.

\textbf{Cross-backbone generalization.}\label{sec:cross_backbone} RR-MoA wins on all 9 backbone$\times$dataset configurations across three architecturally distinct backbones (full table in Appendix~\ref{app:cross_backbone}): MOMENT-small ($d{=}512$, 8 blocks, RevIN), MOMENT-large ($d{=}1024$, 24 blocks, RevIN), and Moirai-1.1-R-small~\citep{woo2024moirai} ($d{=}384$, 6 blocks, no RevIN). Moirai+RR-MoA achieves the best absolute MSE, with Weather reaching $0.209{\pm}0.004$---matching DLinear from scratch ($0.208$). AdaMix on Moirai with last-4 unfreezing retains entropy $1.48$--$1.60$ (no collapse), confirming the failure is RevIN-specific.

\subsection{Top-\texorpdfstring{$k$}{k} Sparse Routing and Task Generalization}
\label{sec:topk}

Top-2 sparse routing retains $63\%$ of the dense mixture's MSE gain on ETTh1 while activating only $2$ of $5$ experts per sample, bounding active expert FLOPs to $40\%$ of the dense setting (full ablation in Appendix~\ref{app:topk}). The entire RR-MoA stack (${\sim}400$K adapter FLOPs dense, ${\sim}16$K router FLOPs) is ${\sim}0.003\%$ of a single MOMENT-small forward pass, so inference overhead is negligible.

\textbf{Multi-horizon and task generalization.} RR-MoA wins \textbf{24/24} configurations across horizons $H \in \{96, 192, 336, 720\}$ on ETTh1 and ETTm1 (3 seeds each), with $-38\%$ to $-51\%$ MSE improvement at every horizon (full grid in Appendix~\ref{app:horizon}). The routing advantage is stable from $H{=}96$ to $H{=}720$. RR-MoA also generalizes to imputation (20\% masked reconstruction, 3 seeds, mean$\pm$std): $-44\%$ to $-67\%$ MSE on 3/3 datasets (Appendix~\ref{app:imputation}).


\section{Conclusion}

We identified \emph{normalization-induced co-adaptation} as the cause of MoE routing collapse on TSFMs, proved via three converging controls and formalized via Proposition~\ref{thm:mi_decomp}, whose quantitative prediction is validated at $\rho{=}{-}0.96$ ($p{<}0.001$) across seven datasets. \textbf{RR-MoA} breaks the collapse by routing on the raw pre-normalization signal with a frozen backbone (\textbf{54/54 wins}, $p{<}0.001$, three backbones, four horizons, two tasks). The \emph{Frozen Paradox}---frozen outperforms unfreezing, formalized in Proposition~\ref{prop:frozen}---reverses the standard PEFT heuristic. More broadly, the normalization-routing tension should apply to any frozen FM with instance-level normalization (RevIN, InstanceNorm) that strips per-sample statistics before the routing path; we validate this in the time series setting and conjecture it extends to other modalities.

\textbf{Limitations.} (1)~Absolute MSE gap to supervised baselines on MOMENT-small (narrows on Moirai). (2)~Decoder-only and native MoE backbones not evaluated. (3)~Proposition~\ref{prop:frozen} is proved for a linear model; the nonlinear case remains open. (4)~Cross-modal generalization is conjectured but not empirically validated.

\section{Proposition Proofs}
\label{app:proofs}

\textbf{Proof of Proposition~\ref{thm:mi_decomp}} (Routing Information Loss Under RevIN).

\begin{proof}
\emph{Part (i)}: The RevIN map $N: \mathbf{x} \mapsto \mathbf{S} = (\mathbf{x} - M)/\Sigma$ is deterministic, so for any router that operates on $\mathbf{S}$ we have the Markov chain $X \to S \to E$. The Data Processing Inequality gives $I(X;E) \geq I(S;E)$.

\emph{Part (ii)}: By the chain rule of mutual information,
\begin{align*}
I(X; E) &= I(M, \Sigma, S;\, E) = I(S;\, E) + I(M, \Sigma;\, E \mid S).
\end{align*}
Under the assumption $(M, \Sigma) \perp\!\!\!\perp S$, the conditional mutual information simplifies:
\begin{align*}
I(M, \Sigma;\, E \mid S) &= H(M, \Sigma \mid S) - H(M, \Sigma \mid E, S) \\
&= H(M, \Sigma) - H(M, \Sigma \mid E, S) = I(M, \Sigma;\, E, S) \\
&= I(M, \Sigma;\, E) + I(M, \Sigma;\, S \mid E) = I(M, \Sigma;\, E)
\end{align*}
where the last step uses $(M,\Sigma) \perp\!\!\!\perp S \mid E$ (which follows from $(M,\Sigma) \perp\!\!\!\perp S$ and the fact that conditioning on $E$, a function of $(M,\Sigma,S)$, cannot create dependence between independent variables). Therefore $I(X;E) - I(S;E) = I(M,\Sigma;\,E)$.

\emph{Part (iii)}: If $E \perp\!\!\!\perp S \mid (M, \Sigma)$, then $E$ is determined entirely by $(M,\Sigma)$: $I(S;\,E) = I(S;\,E \mid M,\Sigma) + I(S;\,E;\,M,\Sigma) - \ldots$. More directly, $I(S;E) = 0$ because $S \to (M,\Sigma) \to E$ forms a Markov chain under this assumption. Therefore $I(X;E) - I(S;E) = I(X;E) = I(M,\Sigma;E) = H(E) - H(E \mid M,\Sigma) = H(E)$ since $(M,\Sigma)$ determine $E$.
\end{proof}

\textbf{Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:hierarchy}} (Strict Head-Level Hierarchy: $\mathcal{C}_\text{head} \subset \mathcal{G}_\text{tmpl} \subset \mathcal{G}_\text{code}$).

\begin{proof}
\emph{$\mathcal{C}_\text{head} \subseteq \mathcal{G}_\text{tmpl}$}: Each $c \in \mathcal{C}_\text{head}$ is, by Definition~\ref{def:discrete_head}, a pooling strategy followed by an MLP head. Both components are expressible as template derivations of the form $S \to \text{Pool}\cdot\text{MLP}\cdot\text{Output}$ (Appendix~\ref{app:grammar}), so $c$ lies in $\mathcal{G}_\text{tmpl}$. \emph{Strict}: $\mathcal{G}_\text{tmpl}$ additionally admits Conv1d pooling and SiLU activations absent from $\mathcal{C}_\text{head}$.

\emph{$\mathcal{G}_\text{tmpl} \subseteq \mathcal{G}_\text{code}$}: Every template derivation produces a valid Python program satisfying Definition~\ref{def:code}. \emph{Strict}: $\mathcal{G}_\text{code}$ contains depthwise convolutions, batch normalization, residual connections, and learned positional weights---none derivable from the template grammar (Table~\ref{tab:expressiveness}).

\emph{Orthogonality of LoRA and bottleneck configurations.} Configurations in $\mathcal{C}\setminus\mathcal{C}_\text{head}$ modify the backbone's attention projections (LoRA) or inject residual modules between encoder blocks (bottleneck). They are not head-level adapters and are therefore not members of $\mathcal{G}_\text{tmpl}$ nor of $\mathcal{G}_\text{code}$ as defined (the code space is restricted to adapter classes operating on extracted hidden states, per Definition~\ref{def:code}). We compare against the LoRA variant separately under the strictly frozen backbone protocol in Table~\ref{tab:baselines}.
\end{proof}

\textbf{Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:frozen}} (Gradient Co-Adaptation Drives Entropy Collapse).

\begin{proof}
Consider a single input $\mathbf{x}$ with backbone $\mathbf{h} = A\mathbf{x}$, router $p = \sigma(\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{h})$, and loss $\ell = p\,\ell_1(\mathbf{h}) + (1-p)\,\ell_2(\mathbf{h})$.

\emph{Part (i): Unfrozen backbone.} The gradient with respect to $A$ is:
\begin{equation*}
\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial A} = p\,\frac{\partial\ell_1}{\partial\mathbf{h}}\mathbf{x}^\top + (1{-}p)\,\frac{\partial\ell_2}{\partial\mathbf{h}}\mathbf{x}^\top + (\ell_1 - \ell_2)\,p(1{-}p)\,\mathbf{w}\mathbf{x}^\top
\end{equation*}
Under gradient descent $\dot{A} = -\eta\,\partial\ell/\partial A$, the induced change in expert~1's loss is:
\begin{equation*}
\dot{\ell}_1\big|_A = \left(\frac{\partial\ell_1}{\partial\mathbf{h}}\right)^\top\dot{A}\,\mathbf{x} \approx -\eta\,p\left\|\frac{\partial\ell_1}{\partial\mathbf{h}}\right\|^2\|\mathbf{x}\|^2
\end{equation*}
and analogously $\dot{\ell}_2\big|_A \approx -\eta\,(1{-}p)\left\|\frac{\partial\ell_2}{\partial\mathbf{h}}\right\|^2\|\mathbf{x}\|^2$. When $p > 1{-}p$ (expert~1 dominates), $\ell_1$ decreases faster than $\ell_2$. The third term in $\partial\ell/\partial A$ further reinforces this: when $\ell_1 < \ell_2$, the factor $(\ell_1 - \ell_2) < 0$ drives $\dot{p} > 0$, pushing $p$ toward~1. Together these create the self-reinforcing loop: dominant expert gets more gradient $\to$ its loss drops faster $\to$ router increases its weight $\to$ it becomes more dominant.

\emph{Part (ii): Frozen backbone.} When $A$ is fixed, $\dot{A} = 0$. The only dynamics are through $\mathbf{w}$:
\begin{equation*}
\dot{\mathbf{w}} = -\eta\,\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial\mathbf{w}} = -\eta\,(\ell_1 - \ell_2)\,p(1{-}p)\,\mathbf{h}
\end{equation*}
This update adjusts routing weights based on current expert performance, but does \emph{not} reshape $\mathbf{h}$ to favor one expert. Without the $A$-mediated feedback loop, there is no systematic bias toward entropy collapse---the router converges to a stable allocation reflecting the static geometry of the fixed representation space.
\end{proof}

\textbf{Budget Sufficiency (Empirical).} Figure~\ref{fig:budget} shows that all 14 tested search budgets $B\in[20,480]$ beat the conv baseline on both ETTh1 and ETTm1 and that the MSE as a function of $B$ saturates around $B\!\approx\!60$. Evolutionary selection did not outperform random sampling in the template space $\mathcal{G}_\text{tmpl}$, indicating that the returns to additional samples past the saturation point are negligible for this space. We report this purely as an empirical observation from the curve; no theoretical sample-complexity bound is claimed.

\section{AAS Search Details}
\label{app:aas_details}

The AAS evolutionary loop maintains a population of 20 adapters over 12 generations (${\sim}240$ total evaluations). Each evaluation performs a 3-epoch fitness probe ($\text{fitness} = -\text{MSE}_\text{val}$) on a single A10G GPU. Elitism preserves the top 2 architectures each generation; 18 new candidates are generated per generation. The population is initialized with 5 hand-designed seed adapters plus 15 from the template grammar. An LLM variant (gpt-4o-mini) generates candidates in the code space $\mathcal{G}_\text{code}$ using evolutionary context (parent code + error feedback). Template-based generation achieves 100\% validity (vs.\ ${\sim}75\%$ for LLM), enabling fair comparison that isolates search space effects from budget differences.

\section{Template Grammar Production Rules}
\label{app:grammar}

\begin{align*}
S &\rightarrow \nonterminal{Pool} \;\cdot\; \nonterminal{MLP} \;\cdot\; \nonterminal{Output} \\
\nonterminal{Pool} &\rightarrow \text{MeanPool} \mid \text{MaxPool} \mid \text{LastToken} \mid \text{AttnPool} \mid \text{Conv1dPool} \\
\nonterminal{MLP} &\rightarrow \nonterminal{Layer} \mid \nonterminal{Layer} \;\cdot\; \nonterminal{MLP} \quad (\text{depth} \leq 3) \\
\nonterminal{Layer} &\rightarrow \text{Linear}(d_\text{in}, d_\text{out}) \;\cdot\; \nonterminal{Act} \;\cdot\; \text{Dropout}(p) \\
\nonterminal{Act} &\rightarrow \text{GELU} \mid \text{ReLU} \mid \text{SiLU} \mid \text{Tanh} \\
\nonterminal{Output} &\rightarrow \text{Linear}(d_\text{last}, H)
\end{align*}
with $d_\text{out} \in \{64, 128, 192, 256, 384\}$ and $p \in \{0.0, 0.1, 0.2, 0.3\}$, yielding $|\mathcal{G}_\text{tmpl}| \approx 1{,}200$.

\label{app:formal_defs}

\begin{definition}[Adapter]
Given a pretrained TSFM backbone $f_\theta: \mathbb{R}^{B \times L} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^{B \times T \times d}$ that maps input time series to encoder hidden states, an \textbf{adapter} is a parameterized module $g_\phi: \mathbb{R}^{B \times T \times d} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^{B \times H}$ that maps hidden states to forecasts, where $\phi \in \Phi_g \subseteq \mathbb{R}^{n_g}$ are trainable weights with $n_g \leq N_\text{max}$ (we set $N_\text{max} = 500{,}000$).
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Architecture Space]
An \textbf{architecture space} $\mathcal{G}$ is a set of adapter specifications, where each $g \in \mathcal{G}$ determines: (i) a parameter space $\Phi_g$ and its dimensionality $n_g$, (ii) a computational graph defining the forward function $g_\phi(\cdot)$, and (iii) an initialization procedure for $\phi$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Discrete Configuration Space $\mathcal{C}$]
The \textbf{discrete space} $\mathcal{C}$ is the Cartesian product of standard adapter hyperparameters:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{C} = \underbrace{\{\text{lin, lora, bn}\}}_{\text{type}} \times \underbrace{\{8,16,32,64\}}_{\text{rank}} \times \underbrace{\{\text{mean, last, max, cls}\}}_{\text{pool}} \times \underbrace{\{\text{lin, mlp1, mlp2}\}}_{\text{head}} \times \cdots
\end{equation}
The full Cartesian product yields ${\sim}2{,}160$ configurations, of which ${\sim}144$ define distinct adapter \textit{computational graphs} when restricted to the head-only subspace.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Head-Only Subspace $\mathcal{C}_\text{head}$]
\label{def:discrete_head}
The \textbf{head-only subspace} $\mathcal{C}_\text{head} \subset \mathcal{C}$ is the restriction of $\mathcal{C}$ to linear-probe configurations (pooling $\to$ MLP head), with LoRA rank $=0$ and no bottleneck.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Code-Level Space $\mathcal{G}_\text{code}$]
\label{def:code}
The \textbf{code space} $\mathcal{G}_\text{code}$ is the set of all Python programs $p$ such that: (i)~$p$ defines a class \texttt{Adapter(nn.Module)} with \texttt{\_\_init\_\_(self, d\_model, output\_dim)} and \texttt{forward(self, h)} mapping $\mathbb{R}^{B \times T \times d} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^{B \times H}$; (ii)~$n_p \leq N_\text{max}$; (iii)~$p$ uses only \texttt{\{torch, nn, F, math\}}.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Template Space $\mathcal{G}_\text{tmpl}$]
\label{def:template}
The \textbf{template space} $\mathcal{G}_\text{tmpl} \subset \mathcal{G}_\text{code}$ is generated by a context-free grammar over 5 pooling types, 4 activations, variable MLP depths and widths, yielding $|\mathcal{G}_\text{tmpl}| \approx 1{,}200$ architectures (Appendix~\ref{app:grammar}).
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Strict Head-Level Hierarchy]
\label{prop:hierarchy}
$\mathcal{C}_\text{head} \subset \mathcal{G}_\text{tmpl} \subset \mathcal{G}_\text{code}$, and both inclusions are strict (proof in Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}).
\end{proposition}

\section{Factorial Decomposition}
\label{app:decomposition}

We decompose the contributions of three factors across 6 evaluation datasets: (1) search space representation (code vs.\ discrete), (2) evolutionary selection pressure, and (3) LLM guidance.

\textbf{Code space $\gg$ discrete space.} All code-space methods outperform discrete evolutionary search (Evo-3epoch, 144 distinct adapter graphs) on 6/6 datasets (Wilcoxon signed-rank $p{=}0.031$, Cohen's $d{=}0.82$). The search space representation is the dominant factor.

\textbf{LLM patterns are individually superior.} Within AAS runs, adapters containing LLM-discovered patterns achieve 19.6\% lower MSE than basic architectures ($p{=}0.003$, Mann-Whitney $U$, $n{=}90$, Cohen's $d{=}0.67$). LLM adapters also use 38\% fewer parameters (mean 110K vs.\ 177K).

\textbf{Validity determines aggregate winner.} The LLM wastes 25\% of evaluations on invalid code; templates achieve 100\% validity.

\textbf{Search budget sufficiency.} We tested 14 budget configurations (20--480 evaluations) across ETTh1 and ETTm1; all beat the conv baseline, even at $B{=}20$ (Appendix~\ref{app:budget}). The curve plateaus around $B\!\approx\!60$.

\section{Deployment-Regime Motivation}
\label{app:deployment}

The frozen-backbone paradigm is motivated by three deployment regimes where full fine-tuning is infeasible:

\textbf{(i) Multi-tenant cloud serving.} A single TSFM sits in GPU VRAM and serves thousands of tenants via lightweight, hot-swappable per-tenant adapters in host RAM. Retraining a separate DLinear per tenant does not amortize the backbone cost and forfeits zero-shot transfer.

\textbf{(ii) On-device and edge deployment.} One backbone is shipped with the device image; per-site adapters are updated over low-bandwidth links. Collecting each site's training data centrally is often impossible for privacy or latency reasons.

\textbf{(iii) Cross-task reuse.} The same frozen backbone drives forecasting, imputation, and classification via different expert pools. We demonstrate the first two tasks explicitly in the main text.
