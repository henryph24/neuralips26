Summary:
This paper studies why mixture of experts adapter heads can fail when adapting frozen time series foundation models. The authors identify normalization induced routing collapse, where a hidden state router collapses to a single expert within a few optimization steps when used with backbones that apply learnable affine instance normalization, such as RevIN in MOMENT. The paper argues that this failure is caused mainly by the router input rather than by the routing optimization itself, since RevIN removes per window mean and scale information that is important for distinguishing temporal regimes. This diagnosis is supported by a mutual information analysis, a training free signal ratio that correlates with collapse risk, and several causal controls, including normalization ablations, comparisons across backbones, and a replication in vision models. As a remedy, the authors propose RR-MoA, which routes using the raw input before normalization while keeping expert adaptation on frozen hidden states. The method is reported to outperform fixed adapters, LoRA, TRACE, AdaMix, and full fine tuning across all evaluated settings, with additional variants used to support generality across multiple backbones, forecasting horizons, and an imputation task.

Response to Reviewer Pm4m

We thank the reviewer for the careful, constructive review, for the close reading down to the router parameter count and α initialization, and for noting that addressing these concerns would raise the score. We address every point below; all supporting results are in the submitted paper unless tagged [NEW].

W1 (framing / DLinear). (Meta-review point 1: "overstate of claims in the paper and requires reframing of contributions.") This is the reviewer's central concern, and we take it seriously. The reviewer is correct that our abstract's headline numbers (54/54; 12-79% over full fine-tuning) are all comparisons within the frozen-adapter/PEFT regime. Those are the right baselines for the frozen-adapter question we ask, and we agree a from-scratch DLinear belongs beside them as a benchmark from outside that regime. Against that benchmark, the reviewer's point holds for RR-MoA-proper: it does trail a from-scratch DLinear on linear-dominated data (~0.68 vs ~0.42 on ETTh1, exactly as noted), because the probe's role is to isolate the router input, not chase accuracy. The variant we deploy differs. Residual-IA+, reported in the submitted Appendix P, matches or beats DLinear rather than trailing it:

65 significant wins vs 11 losses (5.9:1); 107/123 match-or-beat (87%) across 6 backbones × 6 datasets × 4 horizons.
6/6 at H=192; MOMENT-large 23/24 with zero losses (16 significant wins); Moirai-MoE 15/15 (100%).
It dominates non-stationary data at long horizons (ETTm2 -27.0% at H=720; ETTh2 wins at all four).
Even at the single least-favorable case cited (MOMENT-small, H=96), the net mean gap is -2.8%, a win, with ETTh1 the only loss.
We acknowledge that the reviewer is right about our framing; the issue is how we presented the evidence, not the evidence itself. We led with "5/6 match-or-beat" and titled the appendix "Closing the DLinear Gap," which highlighted the single losing cell (ETTh1/H=96). We will fix the presentation in three ways:

Promote the full grid, the 65:11 win-count, and the backbone-contribution analysis (App. P.3) into the main text, as the reviewer asks.
Retitle the section to the outperformance the numbers actually show.
Reframe the abstract's DLinear comparison as a matter of scope, not a weakness, with wording such as: "Because the router isolates the collapse mechanism rather than maximizing accuracy, the minimal RR-MoA probe trails a from-scratch DLinear on linear-dominated datasets; our deployment adapter Residual-IA+ removes this gap, matching or beating DLinear across six backbones and four horizons."
We will present RR-MoA-proper as a diagnostic probe and Residual-IA+ as the deployed method that clears the DLinear bar; the overselling concern is thus one of framing, which we fix.

W2 (Figure 2). We agree the figure is too small and cluttered; we will redraw it larger with non-overlapping modules so it clearly shows the raw-router and frozen-expert paths.

W3 (Moirai gains without collapse). The reviewer is right, and we should have made this clearer. On Moirai there is no collapse, so its 12-29% gain is not collapse-repair. We read it as two results. (1) It confirms the prediction: Moirai has no learnable-affine input normalization, so the co-adaptation that drives collapse cannot occur. (2) Raw routing still helps, for a different reason: Moirai's non-learnable normalization (Kim et al. 2022) also strips per-window location-scale from the hidden states, and the raw input lets the router recover it, a routing effect, not the raw-signal predictiveness the reviewer links to App. P.3: in RR-MoA the raw signal feeds only the router, and experts use frozen hidden states. So the gain cannot come from the Pure Raw-MLP mechanism, where experts read the raw signal directly. So the cross-backbone wins split into two regimes the diagnosis predicts: collapse-repair on learnable-affine RevIN backbones, routing-signal recovery elsewhere. That is a sharper claim than uniform collapse-repair, not a weaker one; the causal chain stands, and we will add the Moirai citation.

W4 (heavy tuning; generalization beyond six). We appreciate this careful read, and believe both concerns have direct answers: the recipe is fixed once and generalizes to unseen data without re-tuning, and the backbone's value is horizon-dependent, near-negligible at H=96 but decisive at H≥192. On the tuning, the 546-run sweep (Appendix P) was a one-time search for one fixed recipe, not a per-dataset cost, so it should not read as a SOTA search, and it gives a win over DLinear (65:11) rather than parity. [NEW] When we apply that recipe unchanged (gate-init b=-2, 5-epoch warmup, shared NLinear, validation early-stop) to three unseen datasets, Residual-IA+ matches or beats DLinear on 8 of 9 cells (Solar 3/3, -9.5%; Traffic 3/3, parity; Exchange 2/3; no collapse).

The reviewer reads App. P.3 as "the raw branch does the work; the TSFM is dead weight," and at H=96 the paper largely agrees: there the backbone's contribution is dataset-dependent and negligible on average. But two things undercut that verdict for the deployed model. First, P.3 measures it against the intermediate Dual-Stream (ETTh2 0.452, ETTm2 0.252), not the deployed Residual-IA+ (ETTh2 0.346, ETTm2 0.188). Second, it is an artifact of the short horizon. [NEW] At H=192, holding the raw branch fixed and toggling only the backbone, the verdict flips outright: Residual-IA+ wins 18/18 (mean -11.8%) on the very ETTh2/ETTm2/Electricity cells P.3 called dead-weight, and still matches or beats a 49K DLinear. So the backbone is not dead weight; at H≥192 it is decisive.

W5 ((μ,σ) tension). (Meta-review point 2: "the (M,Σ)-centric quantity contradicts with paper's ablations.") We understand why this looks like a contradiction; it dissolves once we separate what the router reads from what normalization removes. Normalization is by definition a per-window location-scale map, so it removes exactly (M,Σ); Observation 1 and R(D) are therefore (M,Σ)-centric by necessity, and never claim (M,Σ) is the whole routing signal. R(D) is a ratio: routing signal in the removed part (location-scale) over routing signal in the surviving part (shape). The reviewer's two ablations are exactly that denominator: [μ,σ]-only routing degrades +48% because it drops the surviving shape, and temporal shuffling barely moves performance (≤3% on 4/6, the rest improve) because the signal is order-invariant shape, what normalization leaves. Both populate the denominator, not contradict the numerator. So the theory matches the data: collapse at high R(D), none at low (Traffic R=0.14, where RR-MoA correctly does not improve). We will re-scope Observation 1 as a lower-bound vulnerability predictor, not a claim that (μ,σ) alone is enough.

W6 (minor). The router is 853 params for the reported K=5 model, consistent across the main text, Fig 2, Table A.1, and the code. The K-way head grows with K, so over the expert-count sweep (Table I.3, to K=10) the count reaches 1,100; the Checklist's "1.1K" reflects that larger-K count, not the reported K=5, and we will correct it to 853. On the SR-MoA figure, the 13-42% is over RR-MoA, not the baselines (SR-MoA beats RR-MoA on all six, Table F.2). We will standardize "fine-tuning" and recheck the flagged R(D) parenthesis.

Q1 (input length 512 / horizon 96). The input length is indeed fixed at 512, and the reviewer is right to question it. Multi-horizon results, though, are already in the submitted paper (Table C.1: RR-MoA 12/12 vs best fixed over H∈{96,192,336,720}; Residual-IA+ reaches 6/6 at H=192 in Table P.6); we may not have presented them prominently enough. [NEW] Input-length ablation (L∈{96,192,336,512}, 6 datasets, 3 seeds): no collapse at any L (per-seed entropy 0.89-1.53, far above the 0.00 collapse floor), raw beats normalized routing at all 24 configs (-17% to -84%), and RR-MoA beats the best fixed adapter at 70/72. The answer is yes: both the method and its conclusions hold as the input length and the forecast horizon change, not only at L=512 and H=96.

We will correct the wording.

On significance. The lasting contribution is the diagnosis and its predictor. R(D) is a practical, training-free tool: computed before training, it predicts whether raw routing will help on a new dataset (ρ=-0.88, n=9), and where it will not (Traffic, R=0.14). It transfers beyond our six benchmarks and our router: two distinct variants (SR-MoA, Residual-IA+) confirm it.

We report per-seed results here for length; full tables will follow in the revision if accepted. Given the corrected DLinear framing (Residual-IA+ beats DLinear 65:11, App. P), the re-scoped R(D), and the new input-length ablation, which the reviewer noted would raise the score, we would be grateful for a reconsideration.

The paper, §4.1 (lines 188–190), states that non-learnable I/O scaling "preserves the (M, Σ) statistics in the hidden states the router reads". This is the stated reason regime (ii) is not vulnerable. The rebuttal's response to my W3 asserts the opposite: Moirai's non-learnable normalization "also strips per-window location-scale from the hidden states, and the raw input lets the router recover it." The rebuttal presents the reversal as "a sharper claim, not a weaker one" without acknowledging that it rewrites the paper's stated mechanism. Table N.2's caption meanwhile hedges in the original direction ("hidden states retain enough signal to avoid collapse"), and the rule offered to Reviewer 8b2Z ("a normalizer causes collapse when it strips routing-relevant statistics at the router's input") silently replaces the paper's learnable-affine criterion.

Which claim is correct?

2. "Residual-IA+ matches or beats DLinear" is confounded by NLinear vs. a simplified DLinear
Two facts from the submission itself: Residual-IA+'s shared raw branch is NLinear (App. P.1, fix (v): y = Linear(x − x_T) + x_T), while the calibration anchor is the simplified single-Linear DLinear without trend-seasonal decomposition (App. A, lines 640–643). NLinear's from-scratch advantage over DLinear on level-drifting data is a known result from [17] itself, and the paper's own Batch-4 analysis (lines 1309–1315) shows the same pattern inside the adapter: the NLinear branch helps most on precisely the non-stationary ETT datasets, "matching NLinear's design rationale."

The headline defense of W1/W4 (65 significant wins vs. 11 losses) therefore compares a method whose base predictor is a stronger linear model against a weaker linear anchor. As reported, the grid cannot distinguish "the backbone adds value" from "NLinear beats a simplified DLinear." The [NEW] Exchange result (a ~10× RR-MoA deficit in Table N.4 flipping to 2/3 match-or-beat) is exactly what level anchoring predicts on near-random-walk data, backbone or not.
